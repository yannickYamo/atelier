# How I write a competitive teardown

1. Title the document "Teardown:" plus the rival product's name and the plan tier and version I examined.
2. Directly under the title put a line starting "Examined:" that gives the date range and how I got access (trial, paid seat, public docs).
3. Open with a Bottom Line section, three sentences at most, that says whether they're a threat to us and in which segment.
4. Bottom Line states a Threat Level, and it is one of: Low, Moderate, High, Critical. Put the level in bold.
5. Sections follow in this order: Bottom Line, Who They Sell To, What They Do Well, Where They Fall Short, Pricing, Feature Comparison, Evidence, What We Should Do. What We Should Do goes last.
6. Who They Sell To opens with a single sentence naming their ideal customer by size, role and trigger event.
7. Keep Who They Sell To under 70 words.
8. What They Do Well gets exactly three bullets; forcing three makes me rank instead of list.
9. Lead each of those bullets with a bold label of two to four words, then one sentence of evidence I saw myself.
10. Be fair to the rival: if I can't write What They Do Well with conviction I haven't used the product enough.
11. Where They Fall Short also gets three bullets in the same format, and each must be something a customer would feel, not an engineering nitpick.
12. Never use "clunky", "legacy", "outdated", "inferior" or "killer"; describe what happened when I used it.
13. Don't call them "the competition" or "the enemy"; use their product name every time.
14. Pricing is a table with the columns Plan, Price, Billing Unit and Notable Limit.
15. Copy prices from their public pricing page exactly, currency and period included, and note the date I captured them beneath the table.
16. One row per plan they publish; for an unlisted enterprise tier write "Not published" in Price rather than guessing.
17. Feature Comparison is a table with the columns Capability, Them, Us and Edge.
18. The Edge column takes only: Them, Us, Even.
19. Six to eight rows in Feature Comparison, chosen from what buyers actually ask about in deals, not from our roadmap.
20. In the Them and Us cells write a fact of 8 words or fewer, never a tick or a cross.
21. Don't rig the comparison: at least two rows should show the Edge as Them if that's the truth, and it usually is.
22. Evidence is a numbered list of 4 to 7 sources, each saying what it is, where it came from and the date.
23. Quote customers and reviews word for word in quote marks, with the reviewer's role and the site; never tidy a quote.
24. Every claim in Bottom Line should trace to a numbered Evidence item, cited in square brackets like [3].
25. Separate what I observed from what I infer, and begin every inference with "My read:".
26. Use at most two "My read:" statements in the whole document, or it turns into opinion.
27. What We Should Do is a numbered list of exactly three recommendations, most urgent first.
28. Each recommendation starts with a verb and ends with an owner team and a time frame in brackets, like "(Product, Q3)".
29. At least one recommendation should be something to stop doing or something not to copy.
30. Hold the whole teardown to 400 to 600 words; leadership reads the first screen and the last list.
31. No paragraph longer than three sentences.
32. Keep numbers as they appeared at source: seat counts, prices, limits, and review scores together with the number of reviews.
33. Say what I couldn't verify in one sentence at the end of Evidence, starting "Not verified:".
34. Ask where they will be in a year, not only where they are; a weak product with a fast release cadence is not weak.
35. Use the present tense for what the product does and the past tense for what I did during the test.
36. Refer to our own product by name at least once, and never as "our solution" or "our platform".
37. Give the customer's reason for choosing them in the customer's terms, not in our positioning language.
38. Headings are `##` only, in Title Case, and worded identically from one teardown to the next so they can be compared side by side.
39. Don't describe screenshots in prose; if a screen matters, say what I clicked and what came back in one sentence.
40. Finish the last recommendation and stop: no closing paragraph, no restated summary.
