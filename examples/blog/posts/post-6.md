# We stopped writing a weekly engineering report

It took one engineer roughly three hours a week and I could not find anyone who read it. I asked
nine people directly. Two skimmed the incident section. Nobody had opened it in the previous
month, which I confirmed against the document analytics rather than taking their word for it.

We replaced it with nothing for six weeks, on purpose, to see what people would ask for.

What they asked for was one thing: a list of what shipped, with links. Not status, not risks, not
a narrative. That list is now generated from merged PR titles and takes no human time at all.

The thing I got wrong is that I assumed the report was unread because it was badly written. I
spent two weeks rewriting it before I ran the six-week experiment, and the rewrite changed
readership by zero. The format was not the problem and I wasted the two weeks because I
preferred a problem I knew how to solve.

I do not think this generalizes to every recurring document. A board update has a reader with an
obligation to read it, and that changes the calculus entirely.

The rule I would give is narrower than "kill your status reports." It is: before you improve a
document, confirm somebody is reading the current one.
