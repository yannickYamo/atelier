---
request: Explain how a search engine picks results to someone with no background in it.
---
You type a few words, and answers appear in under a second. That speed hides months of quiet preparation.

Long before you search, the engine sends out software called crawlers. They follow links from page to page across the web, copying what they find. All that text gets sorted into a giant catalogue known as an index. It works like the index at the back of a textbook, listing which pages mention which words.

When your query arrives, the engine does not scan the whole web. It checks the index for pages containing your words, which can still mean millions of candidates. Then the ranking begins.

The engine scores each candidate using many signals. It asks whether the words appear in the title or the main text. It checks how many other trusted sites link to that page, treating links as votes of confidence. It considers how fast the page loads, whether it works on phones, and how fresh the content is. Your location and language matter too.

Modern systems also try to read intent. If you search "jaguar speed", the engine weighs whether you mean the animal or the car. Past behaviour from millions of users helps it guess.

In short, the engine prepares a catalogue in advance, then ranks the matches by relevance and trust.
