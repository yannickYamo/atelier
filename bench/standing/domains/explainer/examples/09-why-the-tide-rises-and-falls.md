---
request: Explain why the tide rises and falls to someone with no background in it.
---
Twice a day, the sea creeps up the beach and then slides back down. The cause sits about 240,000 miles away: the Moon.

The Moon's gravity tugs on everything on Earth, but not equally. Ocean water on the side facing the Moon feels a slightly stronger pull than the planet's centre does. That water bulges outward. On the far side, the solid Earth is pulled away from the water there, so a second bulge forms.

So the ocean is stretched into a long oval, with a hump at each end. Those humps stay roughly lined up with the Moon. Meanwhile, the Earth spins underneath them, carrying your coastline through each hump in turn. Imagine turning around inside a loose jumper while the fabric stays more or less where it is.

When your stretch of coast rotates into a hump, the water piles up and the tide rises. When it rotates out, the tide falls. That gives most places two high tides a day.

The Sun pulls too, though more weakly. When the Sun and Moon line up, their pulls combine and tides run unusually high. When they sit at right angles, tides are gentler.

In short, the tide is the ocean bulging towards the Moon while you spin past.
