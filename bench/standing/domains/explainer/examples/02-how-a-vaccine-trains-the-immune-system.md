---
request: Explain how a vaccine trains the immune system to someone with no background in it.
---
Your body already keeps a defence force on standby. White blood cells patrol constantly, hunting anything that does not belong. When a real germ arrives, they must learn its shape from scratch. That learning takes days, and during those days you get sick.

A vaccine removes the delay. It shows your immune system a harmless preview of the germ. That preview might be a killed virus, a weakened one, or a single protein from its surface. Sometimes it is a set of instructions your own cells use to build that protein. None of these can give you the disease, but your defences react anyway.

Think of a wanted poster circulated through a town before the thief ever arrives. The guards study the face, so recognition later is instant.

During this practice run, your body produces antibodies and, more importantly, memory cells. These memory cells can survive for years, sometimes decades. If the real germ ever turns up, they identify it straight away. The counterattack starts within hours instead of days. Often you clear the infection before you feel any symptoms at all. This is also why some vaccines need a booster: a second viewing sharpens a fading memory.

In short, a vaccine is a safe rehearsal that leaves your immune system ready for the real thing.
