---
request: Explain compound interest to someone with no background in it.
---
Put £100 in a savings account paying 5% a year. After twelve months you have £105. That extra £5 is interest, your reward for leaving the money alone.

Here is where it gets interesting. In year two, the bank pays 5% on £105, not on your original £100. You earn £5.25. That extra 25p is interest earned on interest. That is compounding.

The effect looks tiny early and dramatic later. Imagine a snowball rolling downhill: each turn picks up more snow, and the bigger it grows, the faster it gathers. Your money behaves the same way, provided you keep it rolling.

Time matters even more than the rate. That £100 at 5% grows to roughly £265 after twenty years, and about £700 after forty. You added nothing extra. The gains simply kept earning gains of their own.

The same force can work against you. Credit cards compound too, often monthly, at rates near 20%. Unpaid interest joins your balance, and then that larger balance starts charging you. Debt left alone does not sit still; it accelerates.

So the practical lesson is simple. Save early, even in small amounts, and clear expensive debt quickly.

In short, compound interest means your earnings earn as well. Patience decides whether that works for you or against you.
