---
request: Explain what encryption does to a message to someone with no background in it.
---
When you send a message online, it rarely travels straight to the person you intend. It hops through cables, routers and company servers along the way. Any of those points could, in principle, read what you wrote.

Encryption changes your message so that this stops mattering. It takes your readable words and scrambles them using a mathematical recipe and a secret key. The result looks like meaningless noise. Anyone who intercepts it sees characters with no pattern and no obvious meaning.

Picture a locked metal box travelling by post. The courier carries it, sorts it, perhaps shakes it, but cannot open it. Only the person holding the right key can see inside.

The crucial part is that key. Without it, the scrambling cannot be undone in any practical amount of time. Good modern methods would take a computer longer than the age of the universe to crack by guessing. With the key, unscrambling happens instantly.

It helps to know what encryption does not do. It does not hide the fact that you sent something. It often does not hide who you sent it to, or when. It protects the contents, and little else.

In short, encryption turns your words into nonsense for everyone except the person you chose to read them.
