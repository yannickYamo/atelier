request: Explain how Wi-Fi reaches your laptop to someone with no background in it.
---
Your laptop never really touches the internet. It touches a small box in your home: the router. The internet itself arrives through a physical cable buried under your street, carrying data as electrical pulses or flashes of light. That cable ends at your router, and the router's job is to translate.

What it translates into is radio waves. These are invisible ripples of energy that travel through air, through walls, and across rooms. Your router pushes them out in every direction, thousands of times a second. A tiny antenna inside your laptop catches them and converts the pattern back into data. Your laptop then replies using waves of its own.

Imagine two people in a dark room, flicking torches on and off in an agreed rhythm. Neither one can see the other's face, yet a full message still gets across. The agreed rhythm is what matters, not the light itself.

Wi-Fi works the same way, just unimaginably faster. The waves carry rapid on-off patterns that both devices have agreed to interpret identically. Routers also use separate frequency channels, so your neighbour's network doesn't scramble yours. Walls and distance absorb the energy, which is why your signal fades upstairs.

In short, your router turns cable signals into radio, and your laptop listens and answers back.
