---
request: Explain inflation to someone with no background in it.
---
Every year, the same shopping basket tends to cost a little more. That steady rise in prices is what economists call inflation.

It is measured by tracking the cost of a typical set of goods and services. Food, rent, fuel, haircuts, bus fares — all of it counts. If that basket costs three percent more than last year, inflation is three percent.

The important part is what this does to your money. When prices climb, each pound or dollar you hold buys slightly less. Imagine your savings as ice cubes left in a warm room; they look intact, but they quietly shrink.

Why does it happen? Sometimes people want to buy more than businesses can supply, so sellers raise their prices. Sometimes costs jump — oil, wages, shipping — and firms pass that on to you. Sometimes there is simply too much money chasing the same amount of stuff.

Mild inflation is normal, and most central banks aim for around two percent a year. They raise interest rates to cool spending when prices rise too quickly. Falling prices carry risks too, because shoppers delay purchases and the economy stalls.

In short, inflation is the slow erosion of what your money can buy.
